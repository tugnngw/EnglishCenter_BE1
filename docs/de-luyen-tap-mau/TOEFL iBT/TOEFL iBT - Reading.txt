[PASSAGE] The Spread of Paper
Before the invention of paper, people wrote on many different materials. In ancient Egypt, writers used papyrus, a material made from the stems of a water plant. In Europe, parchment, which was made from animal skin, was common for important documents. Both materials had disadvantages: papyrus cracked easily in dry climates, and parchment was extremely expensive to produce.
Paper as we know it was developed in China around the second century CE. According to tradition, an official named Cai Lun improved earlier methods by mixing tree bark, old fishing nets and pieces of cloth with water, then pressing the mixture into thin sheets and drying them. The new material was light, cheap and easy to store, and it quickly replaced bamboo strips and silk for everyday writing.
For several centuries, the technique remained a Chinese secret. It reached the Islamic world in the eighth century, and the city of Baghdad soon became a major center of paper production. From there, papermaking spread to North Africa and Spain, and eventually to the rest of Europe by the thirteenth century.
The arrival of cheap paper had important consequences. When the printing press was invented in Europe in the fifteenth century, there was already enough paper to print large numbers of books. As a result, books became affordable for many more people, which helped to spread new ideas in science, religion and politics.
